Start here

Effects first, causes later

先看到結果，再回頭找原因

Most of science runs in one direction. You know the cause, and you predict the effect. But some of the most useful questions run the other way. You can see the effect, and you have to figure out what caused it. Mathematicians call this an inverse problem, and once you learn to spot one, you'll find them everywhere.

Part 1 of 8

Two directions

順著做 vs. 倒著推

Imagine you have a recipe. You follow the steps, and out comes a cake. That's the forward problem: from causes to effects. It's usually straightforward, even if the cake doesn't turn out perfect.

Now flip it around. Someone hands you a slice of cake and asks, "What went into this?" You can taste sugar, and maybe a hint of lemon. But how many eggs? How long was it in the oven? You're working backwards, and suddenly the job is much harder.

That's an inverse problem in a nutshell. The effect is right in front of you. The cause is hidden, and you have to piece it together from clues.

Part 2 of 8

Why backwards is hard

倒推為什麼難：三個考驗

In the early twentieth century, the French mathematician Jacques Hadamard spelled out what makes a problem behave nicely. He called such a problem well-posed, and it has to pass three tests. First, there has to be an answer. Second, there should be only one. Third, the answer shouldn't jump around when the data change a tiny bit.

TEST 1
An answer exists存在性：至少有一個答案
TEST 2
Only one answer唯一性：答案只有一個
TEST 3
Small wobble, small change穩定性：資料小變動，答案也只小變動

Forward problems usually pass all three. Inverse problems often fail at least one, and that's what makes them tricky.

Think about shadows. A ball casts a round shadow. But so does a coin held face-on to the light. If all you have is the shadow, you can't tell which one you're looking at. More than one cause fits the same effect.

The third test is sneakier. Real measurements always come with a little noise. In many inverse problems, that tiny bit of noise gets blown up into huge errors in the answer. It's like trying to sharpen a blurry photo: push too hard, and you end up with a picture full of speckles.

Part 3 of 8

Seeing inside the body

CT 掃描：從一堆影子重建切面

The most famous inverse problem might be sitting in your local hospital.

An X-ray picture is basically a shadow. The rays pass through your body, and bone and tissue soak up some of them along the way. Everything along each ray gets squashed into a single number, so a rib in front and a rib behind end up on top of each other.

A CT scanner gets around this by taking X-ray shadows from many angles, all the way around you. One angle on its own doesn't tell you much. Put hundreds of them together, though, and a computer can work backwards to rebuild a cross-section, a "slice," of what's inside.

Funnily enough, the math was ready long before the machine. In 1917, the Austrian mathematician Johann Radon showed how to recover a function from its line integrals, which is basically all of its shadows. Decades later, the physicist Allan Cormack worked out how this could be used for medical images, and the engineer Godfrey Hounsfield built the first practical scanner at the British company EMI. The first patient was scanned in London in October 1971. In 1979, Cormack and Hounsfield shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Part 4 of 8

Listening to the Earth

用地震波「聽」出地心

Nobody can dig down to the center of the Earth to take a look. We can't even see through a few meters of rock, let alone thousands of kilometers of it. So how do we know what's down there? We listen.

When a large earthquake hits, seismic waves travel through the planet and are picked up by instruments all over the world. The waves speed up, slow down and bend depending on what they pass through. By comparing when and where they arrive, seismologists can work backwards to the structure inside.

In the 1930s, the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann noticed something odd. Faint waves were showing up in a zone where, according to the accepted model, there shouldn't have been any. In 1936, she proposed an explanation: inside the Earth's core sits a smaller inner core, and the waves were bouncing off it. Later work showed that this inner core is solid. Her paper had a famously short title: just P′.

Part 5 of 8

Can you hear the shape of a drum?

聽聲音，能不能聽出鼓的形狀？

Here's a question that sounds almost like a riddle. Suppose you could hear every note a drum can possibly make. Could you work out its exact shape?

The mathematician Mark Kac asked this in a famous 1966 paper, "Can One Hear the Shape of a Drum?" The sound tells you quite a lot. From the notes alone, you can work out the drum's area and even the length of its edge. So it seemed plausible that the full set of notes pins down the shape completely.

It took until 1992 for the answer to arrive, and the answer was no. Carolyn Gordon, David Webb and Scott Wolpert found two drums with different shapes that make exactly the same set of notes. If all you could hear were the notes, you'd have no way to tell them apart. Even with perfect data, this inverse problem has more than one answer.

Part 6 of 8

The art of a good guess

資料不夠時，加一點合理的假設

So if the data alone can't give us a single, stable answer, what do we do? We add something the data don't contain: a sensible expectation about what the answer should look like.

Suppose you have a few measured points, and you want the curve they came from. A wild zigzag can pass through every single point. So can a gentle, smooth curve. Both fit the data. But in most real situations, the smooth one is a far better bet.

This idea has a name: regularization. In effect, you tell the math, "Among all the answers that fit, give me the calmest one." If you've studied statistics, you'll recognize the same spirit in a Bayesian prior. It isn't cheating. It's admitting that the data can't do the job alone, and being upfront about what you assume.

Part 7 of 8

A photo of a black hole

黑洞照片其實是「算」出來的

On April 10, 2019, the Event Horizon Telescope team showed the world the first image of a black hole: the giant one at the center of the galaxy M87.

No single telescope is big enough to see it. So the team linked radio telescopes around the globe into one virtual telescope roughly the size of the Earth. The catch is that a telescope like this only collects scattered pieces of the full picture. Everything in between has to be filled in by computation, which makes it a classic inverse problem.

To make sure the result wasn't just their assumptions talking, the scientists split into four teams that worked independently, without talking to each other. They all ended up with the same basic picture: a bright, lopsided ring around a dark center.

Part 8 of 8

You do this every day

其實你每天都在解反問題

Inverse problems aren't just for scientists. A doctor hears a cough and thinks about what might be causing it. A mechanic listens to a strange noise and guesses what's broken. You notice a friend going quiet at dinner and wonder what's on their mind.

Each time, you face the same troubles Hadamard pointed out. The clues might not be enough. Several causes could explain them. And one small misreading could send you off in the wrong direction.

The scientific answer is also the everyday one. Collect clues from more than one angle. Use what you already know. And hold your conclusion a little loosely. That's how a CT scanner sees inside you, and it's also how good doctors, detectives and friends think.

Natural English

語感筆記

這篇裡最值得帶走的 12 個說法。點英文例句可以聽發音。

work backwards

倒推。重點在「一步一步往回推」，常接 from：從某個終點往回推。

Let's work backwards from the deadline and see when we need to start.

我們從截止日往回推，看看什麼時候要開始。

figure out / work out

都是「想出來」。figure out 偏「搞懂」，對象可以是人或情況（I can't figure him out.）；work out 更有「計算、推導」的味道，也可以表示事情最後「順利解決」。

Don't worry, it'll all work out.

別擔心，最後都會沒事的。

turn out / end up

turn out 看的是「結果揭曉」，常帶一點意外；end up 看的是「最後落在哪裡」，常是沒計畫的。

It turned out the museum was closed, so we ended up at a café.

結果博物館沒開，我們最後跑去咖啡廳。

let alone

前半句通常是否定，後面接「更不可能」的事。

I can barely order coffee in French, let alone ask for directions.

我連用法文點咖啡都有困難，更別說問路了。

pin down

把模糊的東西釘住、確定下來：原因、時間、某人的立場都可以。

It's hard to pin down exactly when the noise started.

很難確定那個聲音到底是什麼時候開始的。

tell … apart

tell 當「分辨」時，幾乎都跟 can / can't 一起出現。

I still can't tell the twins apart.

我到現在還是分不出這對雙胞胎。

The catch is…

好處講完之後，帶出隱藏的限制。問句 What's the catch? 非常常用。

The hotel is cheap. The catch is that it's an hour from the city.

飯店很便宜，問題是離市區要一個小時。

get around

不是正面解決，而是找路繞開。

We got around the language barrier with a translation app.

我們用翻譯 app 繞過了語言障礙。

… talking

把「說話的人」換成「背後的原因」：是某個東西讓你這樣說、這樣想。

Don't take it personally. That's just the jet lag talking.

別放在心上，那只是時差害的。

your best bet

口語給建議的句型，比 You should 柔和，帶「依我判斷」的語氣。

Your best bet is to take the train.

你最好的選擇是搭火車。

go + adjective

表示狀態「變成」，多半是突然的或不好的變化：go quiet、go wrong、go missing。

Everyone went quiet when the manager walked in.

經理一走進來，大家都安靜了。

soak up

原意是吸收，像海綿吸水。旅遊時很常用來說「好好感受」陽光、氣氛。

We spent the afternoon soaking up the sun on the beach.

我們整個下午都在海灘上曬太陽。
